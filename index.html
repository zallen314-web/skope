What it does

Four ways to look closer at any US stock, ETF or portfolio.

1) Factor models

See what really drives your returns. Factor exposures show how much comes from the overall market, company size, value versus growth, profitability, investment style and momentum, and how much is left over.

2) Risk analysis

See how your portfolio would hold up when markets turn: what-if scenarios you set yourself, how it reacts to interest rates, oil and the dollar, how it has behaved in down markets, and which holdings carry the most risk.

3) Fundamental views

Know what you're paying for each holding: valuation multiples, margins and growth from SEC filings, each compared with a year ago, plus highlights from the latest earnings and the company's key risks.

4) Weekly updates

Stay up to date with portfolio performance, risk and headlines, sent to your inbox every week or month.

Worked example: beating the market without alpha

A hypothetical portfolio returned 14.8% a year over five years while the S&P 500 returned 11.5%. That looks like 3.3 points of outperformance. Here is how Skope breaks it down.

Momentum (MOM)0.35+0.5%Tilted toward recent winners while they kept winning.

Every point of the outperformance is explained by known exposures. None of it is alpha.

The takeaway: the portfolio beat the market because it held more of the market, plus a few style bets that happened to pay off. A low-cost index fund with about 30% more market exposure would have produced nearly the same result. Genuine skill shows up as positive alpha with a t-stat of 2 or more, across a long enough period.

Checking your own portfolio

  1. Performance: compare the return to the benchmark. Beating it is the start of the question, not the answer.
  2. Factors: check market beta. Above 1 means amplified market exposure; that alone can explain outperformance in a rising market.
  3. Attribution: see how much of the return came from factors and how much is left as alpha.
  4. Alpha: check its t-stat. Below 2, it can't be told apart from luck.
  5. Risk: compare volatility, Sharpe, drawdown and downside capture with the benchmark. Similar Sharpe with higher volatility means more risk, not more skill.
  6. Scenarios: see what a market drop would do to the portfolio before it happens.

How to read a factor regression

Skope explains a stock's or portfolio's excess return (its return above cash) with a set of factor returns:

Rₜ − RFₜ = α + βMkt·(Mkt − RF)ₜ + βSMB·SMBₜ + βHML·HMLₜ + … + εₜ

Each β (beta) is a sensitivity: how much the return has moved, on average, for a 1% move in that factor, holding the others fixed. α (alpha) is the average return the factors don't explain. ε is everything else: company-specific news.

Example: what a result looks like and how to read it
Factorβt-statReading
Market1.1218.4Moves about 1.12% for each 1% market move. Strongly significant.
Size (SMB)−0.21−2.6Behaves like a large-cap stock (negative = large, positive = small).

R² = 0.78: the factors explain 78% of the month-to-month variation; the other 22% is company-specific. Skope shows significant betas in bold.
t-stat
The estimate divided by its standard error. Roughly, |t| ≥ 2 means the effect is statistically significant at the 5% level (unlikely to be zero). t = β ÷ SE(β)
p-value
The chance of seeing an estimate this far from zero if the true value were zero. Below 0.05 is the usual cutoff for "significant." Stars: * < 0.10, ** < 0.05, *** < 0.01.
Standard error & 95% confidence interval
How precise an estimate is. The true value lies within about ±1.96 standard errors of the estimate 95% of the time. 95% CI = β ± 1.96 × SE
R² and adjusted R²
Share of the return's variation the factors explain. Adjusted R² penalizes adding factors that don't help. Diversified funds often show 0.9+, single stocks much less.
Alpha (α)
Average return left over after the factors. Shown annualized: monthly α × 12, or daily α × 252. Positive and significant alpha is evidence of skill, or of a factor the model leaves out.
Return attribution
How much each factor contributed to the average return: its beta times the factor's average return over the period, annualized. The pieces plus alpha add up to the average excess return. contribution = β × average factor return × 12

The factors

Factor returns come from the Kenneth R. French Data Library. Each is a long-short portfolio of US stocks, so it captures one style while staying roughly market-neutral.

Market (Mkt − RF)
The whole US stock market's return minus the risk-free rate (one-month Treasury bills). β above 1 amplifies market moves; below 1 dampens them.
Size (SMB, small minus big)
Small companies minus large ones. Positive β behaves like a small-cap stock; negative like a large-cap.
Value (HML, high minus low)
Cheap stocks (high book-to-market) minus expensive ones. Positive β = value tilt; negative = growth tilt.
Profitability (RMW, robust minus weak)
Highly profitable companies minus weakly profitable ones. Positive β = quality-like behavior.
Investment (CMA, conservative minus aggressive)
Companies that grow assets slowly minus those that expand fast. Positive β = conservative investors; negative = aggressive growth spenders.
Momentum (MOM)
Stocks with the best returns over the past 12 months (skipping the latest month) minus the worst, rebalanced monthly. Positive β means the return moves with recent winners. Owning stocks that went up is not the same thing: momentum keeps rotating into whatever is leading now.
Factor models
CAPM: market only. Fama-French 3: market, size, value. Fama-French 5: adds profitability and investment. FF5 + momentum: adds momentum.

Returns & risk

Annualized return
Average yearly growth rate over the period, compounded.
Volatility
Standard deviation of returns, annualized. About two-thirds of years land within ±1 volatility of the average. σannual = σmonthly × √12 (daily: × √252)
Sharpe ratio
Return above cash per unit of volatility. Above 1 is strong; the long-run US market is around 0.4–0.5. Sharpe = (average return − risk-free rate) ÷ volatility
Max drawdown
The largest peak-to-trough fall over the period.
VaR 5% (value at risk)
A bad-but-not-worst period: returns were worse than this only 5% of the time.
CVaR 5% (expected shortfall)
The average return in that worst 5% of periods. Always worse than VaR.
Skewness
Lopsidedness. Negative means occasional large losses; positive means occasional large gains.
Excess kurtosis
Fat tails. Above 0 means extreme moves happen more often than a normal bell curve predicts.
Beta vs benchmark
Average move for a 1% move in the benchmark (SPY, QQQ or IWM), from a one-factor regression. Unlike the multi-factor market beta, it lets other styles move along with the market.
Tracking error
Volatility of the return difference against the benchmark: how far the portfolio wanders from it. TE = σ(portfolio − benchmark), annualized
Information ratio
Average outperformance per unit of tracking error. IR = annualized (portfolio − benchmark) ÷ TE
Risk contribution
Each holding's share of total portfolio volatility, after accounting for how holdings move together. A holding can be 15% of the weight but 30% of the risk. sharei = wi × (Σw)i ÷ σ²portfolio
Diversification ratio
Weighted average of the holdings' volatilities divided by the portfolio's volatility. 1 means no diversification benefit; higher is better.
Effective number of holdings
How many equal-sized positions the portfolio behaves like. Neff = 1 ÷ Σ wi²
Rolling exposures
Factor betas re-estimated over moving 36-month windows, to show how a style has drifted over time.

Scenarios & macro

What-if scenario
Estimated one-month impact of shocks you set, from past sensitivities. Not a forecast: it leaves out company-specific news. impact ≈ βmarket × market shock + Σ sensitivity × (shock beyond what the market move implies)
"Beyond the market"
The part of a macro move that a market move of that size doesn't usually bring with it. This keeps the market's effect from being counted twice.
95% range
The span the estimate falls in given the uncertainty in the sensitivities. Wide ranges mean the history doesn't pin the effect down well.
Macro sensitivities
How much the return has moved with a 1-point rise in the 10-year Treasury yield, a 10% rise in oil or the dollar, or a 1-point widening in credit spreads, holding the market's move fixed. Data from FRED.
Downside / upside capture
Average return in the benchmark's down months as a share of the benchmark's average loss, and the same for up months. 80% downside with 100% upside is a favorable asymmetry.

Valuation

Financial figures come from SEC filings, summed over the trailing twelve months (TTM), and combined with the live price.

TTM (trailing twelve months)
The last four quarters added together: the latest fiscal year plus this year's quarters minus the same quarters a year earlier.
Market cap
Share price × shares outstanding.
P/E (price-to-earnings)
What you pay per $1 of annual profit. P/E = market cap ÷ TTM net income
PEG (trailing)
P/E adjusted for growth, using last year's actual EPS growth. Around 1 suggests the multiple is in line with growth. PEG = P/E ÷ (EPS growth × 100)
P/S (price-to-sales)
market cap ÷ TTM revenue Useful when earnings are small or negative.
P/B (price-to-book)
market cap ÷ shareholders' equity
EV/EBITDA
Enterprise value (market cap + debt − cash) over operating earnings before depreciation and amortization. Compares companies with different debt levels. Not meaningful for banks.
Free cash flow yield
Cash generated after capital spending, as a share of market cap. FCF yield = (operating cash flow − capex) ÷ market cap
Gross / operating / net margin
Gross profit, operating income and net income as a share of revenue.
ROE (return on equity)
TTM net income ÷ shareholders' equity
EPS (diluted)
Earnings per share, counting shares from options and convertibles.
Revenue growth (YoY / TTM)
YoY compares the latest quarter with the same quarter last year. TTM compares the last twelve months with the twelve before.
